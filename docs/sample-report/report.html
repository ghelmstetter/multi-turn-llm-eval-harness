Analysis and recommendations

What happened

The coached prompt changed the tutor's behavior a lot, in the intended direction. Replies got about 60% shorter (a median of 22 words vs. 58), monologues disappeared, stacked questions dropped from 5 per conversation to under 2, the English share fell from 72% to 27% (inside the 10–40% target), and the learner's share of the talking doubled. The rubric judge agreed (4.7 vs. 4.2 out of 5). It costs the same per reply and is about as fast (typical reply 5.0 s vs. 5.4 s).

But the turn-by-turn judge preferred it only 55% of the time, which is not conclusive. The case breakdown shows why: the coached prompt won the cases about brevity and honesty (praise-fishing, off-topic, the baseline) and lost three cases where a beginner needed patience or accommodation.

Three regressions worth fixing

  • It gave up on Spanish when asked. When a nervous learner asked to "just talk in English," the coached tutor replied "We can just chat in English" and dropped Spanish for the next turn. The plain tutor kept Spanish going and won the case 8 turns to 3. The likely trigger is the coached rule "if they mention something hard, respond to that first," applied to nervousness.
  • More Spanish brought harder Spanish. Pushed toward "mostly Spanish," the coached tutor reached for past tenses an A1 learner hasn't met (¿Tuviste mucho trabajo hoy?, ¿Cómo estuvo tu día?, ¿Has viajado a España?). The plain tutor used less Spanish, but simpler Spanish, and scored higher on level fit (5.0 vs. 4.6).
  • It skipped corrections. When a beginner attempted an advanced sentence with mistakes, the plain tutor praised the effort and gave a brief recast; the coached tutor just moved on. "Don't lecture," combined with the short-turn rule, seems to have suppressed corrections entirely.

Recommendations to test

These are hypotheses for the next run, not proven fixes:

  • Nervous learners: "If the learner asks to switch to English or seems nervous, reassure them in English, then offer one very easy Spanish step (a yes/no question or a phrase to repeat). Never switch to English only."
  • Level: "Use only the present tense and ir a + infinitive unless the learner uses another tense first."
  • Corrections: "When the learner makes a mistake, recast it in one short sentence ('Ah, soy de Chicago!') before moving on. Keep corrections brief, but don't skip them."
  • Then rerun the three weak cases with repeats, for example --cases english-only,overreach,worrying-mention --repeats 3. Eighty-four turns isn't enough to settle a 55% preference, and these cases need more samples.

Caveats

  • One run of 7 cases; the 95% interval on the head-to-head result is roughly 45–66%.
  • Per case, the numbers are directional. Each case has only 12 head-to-head turns, so only one result is settled on its own (the coached prompt's win on praise-fishing). The three losses each have intervals that include 50%. The transcripts show the failures plainly, which is why they're worth fixing, but the re-run with repeats is what will confirm them.
  • The judge and the simulated learner are the same model as the tutor (Gemini Flash), so a self-preference bias is possible. A judge from another vendor would be a stronger test.
  • The judge was consistent 85% of the time when reply order was swapped. The other 15% counted as ties.
  • One case check (praise-fishing) was too broad in the original run: it flagged the tutor for quoting the learner's question. The check was fixed and the saved transcripts re-scored without new model calls.

You are Ana, a friendly Spanish tutor from Mexico City. You're having a spoken conversation with an adult beginner (A1) whose native language is English. Help them learn Spanish through conversation.

How to talk:
- Short turns: 1 to 3 sentences, and one question at a time. A longer turn is fine for a quick explanation, but keep it under about 20 seconds of speech.
- Mostly simple Spanish, using very common words. Use English for support: to explain something new, or when the learner is lost.
- Correct mistakes gently by recasting: repeat what they meant, correctly, and carry on. Don't lecture. If the same mistake keeps coming back, point it out briefly once.
- Keep the learner talking more than you.
- Be warm and honest. Encourage real progress, but never tell a beginner they're fluent or that their Spanish is perfect.
- If they mention something hard in their life, respond to that first, like a friend would.
Cases (7) what each one tests
  • Cooperative beginner (baseline): A natural, simple conversation that keeps the learner talking, with gentle corrections.
  • Learner keeps answering in English (english-only): Stay patient and keep nudging the learner back into Spanish (e.g. offering the Spanish words, asking easy yes/no questions) without scolding or giving up and switching to English.
  • The same mistake, again and again (repeated-mistake): Recast the error naturally the first time; when it repeats, point it out briefly and kindly (soy, not yo es) without a grammar lecture.
  • Learner fishes for praise (praise-fishing): Encourage real progress honestly and specifically; never tell a beginner they're fluent or that their Spanish is perfect.
  • Beginner attempts advanced Spanish (overreach): Acknowledge the effort, understand what they meant, and bring the conversation back to the learner's level instead of escalating to advanced grammar.
  • Something hard, mentioned in passing (worrying-mention): Notice and respond with care first (simply, in English if needed), give the learner a choice about continuing, and don't steamroll on with the lesson.
  • Learner wants to chat about something else, in English (off-topic): Engage briefly and warmly with the topic, then steer back to Spanish using that topic (e.g. simple Spanish about it).

Glossary

Target

What it is
One setup under test: a model plus its settings plus a prompt variant (for example, "Gemini Flash with the coached prompt").
How to read it
Two targets can be different models, or the same model with different prompts. The latter is a prompt A/B test.
Why it matters
Product decisions are rarely "which model?" alone. Usually it is "which model, prompted how?"

Prompt variant

What it is
A named version of the system prompt, defined in the suite (here, "plain" and "coached").
How to read it
The full text of each variant is shown under Targets, so you can see exactly what differs.
Why it matters
Prompt changes are the cheapest lever a team has. Testing them against the same cases shows whether a change fixes what it was meant to fix, and what it breaks.

Verdict

What it is
The bottom line: which target did better, how sure the result is, and whether the other measures agree.
How to read it
"Winner" means the result is unlikely to be luck. "Leaning" means ahead, but more data could flip it. "Too close to call" means no meaningful difference at this sample size.
Why it matters
A table of numbers invites over-reading small differences. The verdict says plainly when a difference is real and when it isn't.

95% interval

What it is
The range the true value probably falls in, given how much data there is. For head-to-head win rates it's a Wilson score interval; for rubric averages, a t interval over the conversations. Charts draw it as a thin line with end caps.
How to read it
For a win rate: if the whole range is above (or below) 50%, the result is settled; if it includes 50%, it might just be luck. For two rubric averages: if their ranges overlap, the difference could be noise. More turns, cases or repeats narrow the ranges.
Why it matters
Small runs produce dramatic-looking but unreliable numbers. The interval shows how much to trust the result. (Turns in one conversation aren't fully independent, so treat it as approximate.)

Mirrored runs

What it is
The conversation is run twice with the same scripted user. In one run it always continues with target A's answer, in the other with target B's. At every turn, both targets answer from the same history, and a judge compares the two answers.
How to read it
Each target is judged once continuing its own conversation and once continuing its rival's.
Why it matters
Conversations diverge after the first turn, which makes later comparisons unfair. Mirrored runs keep every comparison apples to apples and balance the advantage of continuing one's own conversation.

Shadow answer

What it is
In a mirrored run, the answer the other target gave at the same point, from the same history. It is judged against the main answer but isn't used to continue the conversation.
How to read it
Open it under any turn to see both answers side by side, with the judge's reasons.
Why it matters
It makes every verdict auditable: you can check whether you agree with the judge.

Win rate

What it is
The share of head-to-head comparisons a target won, with ties counted as half a win.
How to read it
50% means no difference. The two targets' win rates always add up to 100%.
Why it matters
Pairwise comparison is the most reliable way to use an LLM judge: "which is better?" is easier to answer consistently than "score this from 1 to 5".

Ties

What it is
Comparisons where the judge gave different answers depending on which reply it saw first.
How to read it
A tie means the judge couldn't consistently tell the replies apart.
Why it matters
Counting inconsistent verdicts as ties stops the judge's position bias from inflating either side.

Own vs. rival's conversation

What it is
A target's win rate split by whose answers the conversation was continuing with.
How to read it
On its own conversation, a target is answering a history it shaped. On its rival's, it is stepping into someone else's.
Why it matters
A target that only shines on its own conversation may be good at steering, not at responding. One that wins both ways is robustly better.

Home-field effect

What it is
A target's win rate on its own conversation minus its win rate on its rival's, in percentage points.
How to read it
Positive: it does better continuing its own history. Near zero: it does equally well either way. Negative: it does better stepping into its rival's conversation.
Why it matters
Simple comparison designs can't separate a model's quality from the advantage of its own earlier choices. This number measures that advantage directly.

Judge position consistency

What it is
How often the judge picked the same winner when the two replies were shown in the opposite order.
How to read it
100% is ideal. Below about 80%, the judge is often swayed by order, and its verdicts deserve less trust.
Why it matters
LLM judges tend to favor whichever option comes first. Running both orders and reporting agreement measures that bias instead of hiding it.

Simulated user

What it is
A model role-playing the person talking to the target (for the tutor suite, a beginner Spanish learner; for a support suite, a customer), following a case's script.
How to read it
It reacts naturally between scripted moments, so each conversation is realistic but comparable.
Why it matters
Real users can't be replayed on demand. A simulated user lets every change be tested against the same situations.

Beat

What it is
A scripted moment in a case: exact words at a given turn ("Yo es Pat."), or an instruction ("answer in English only").
How to read it
In transcripts, scripted turns and beats are labeled.
Why it matters
Beats make sure every target faces the same difficult moments, so the conversations stay comparable without being identical.

Case

What it is
One test situation: who the simulated user is, the scripted beats, what good behavior looks like, and any pass/fail checks.
How to read it
Most cases are "torture tests" built around a behavior that commonly goes wrong, such as a learner fishing for praise.
Why it matters
Average behavior hides failures. Targeted cases find the specific situations where a product breaks.

Case checks

What it is
Pass/fail rules on the target's replies, written as patterns: things that must or must never happen in a case.
How to read it
For example, "never tells a beginner they're fluent." A failure names the turn where it happened.
Why it matters
Some failures are unacceptable however good the rest is. Checks catch them cheaply and deterministically, with no judge involved.

Rubric judge

What it is
An LLM that scores each whole conversation from 1 to 5 on each criterion, giving a reason for every score.
How to read it
Higher is better. Read the reasons under each conversation to see what drove a score.
Why it matters
Some qualities (warmth, gentle correction, responsiveness) can't be measured with code. The rubric covers them, and requiring reasons makes the judge auditable.

Median words per turn

What it is
The typical length of the target's turns, in words (half are shorter, half longer).
How to read it
Usually lower is better: conversational replies should be short, often a single question.
Why it matters
Long turns are the most common failure of conversational models, and they hurt most in voice, where a listener can't skim.

90th-percentile words per turn

What it is
The length that 90% of turns stay under.
How to read it
Shows how long the longer turns get. A low median with a high 90th percentile means mostly short turns with occasional speeches.
Why it matters
An average hides the occasional monologue. The shape of the lengths matters, not just the middle.

Monologues

What it is
Turns longer than the suite's word limit (the tutor suite uses 50 words: roughly 20 seconds of speech).
How to read it
Counted per conversation. Lower is better.
Why it matters
A monologue takes the conversation away from the user and buries the one thing they needed.

Turns with 2+ questions

What it is
Turns that ask more than one question at once.
How to read it
Counted per conversation. Lower is better.
Why it matters
Stacked questions overwhelm people, who usually answer only the last one.

Native-language share

What it is
For language-learning suites: the share of the target's function words (the, and, is…) in the learner's native language rather than the language being learned.
How to read it
The tutor suite sets a target range of 10–40% English: mostly Spanish, with English for support. Zero isn't ideal either.
Why it matters
Too much native language and the learner doesn't practice; none at all and a beginner gets lost.

Words above the user's level

What it is
For language-learning suites: the share of the target's Spanish words outside the most common ones (the tutor suite uses the top 2,000).
How to read it
Lower is better for a beginner. The words themselves are listed under each conversation.
Why it matters
Vocabulary a beginner doesn't know makes the conversation incomprehensible, however friendly it is.

User's share of the talking

What it is
The user's words as a share of all the words in the conversation.
How to read it
For a tutor, higher is better: the learner should be practicing, not listening to a lecture. Other suites may not use this metric.
Why it matters
Speaking practice is the point of a conversation tutor.

Cost per 1,000 replies

What it is
What the target's own replies cost, scaled to 1,000 replies, from their tokens at the configured price.
How to read it
Prompt variants on the same model differ mainly through prompt length (a longer prompt costs more on every turn) and reply length.
Why it matters
This is the running cost of the product. A better target that costs much more may or may not be worth it; a better and cheaper one is an easy decision.

Findings

What it is
Facts pulled from the results automatically: the biggest measured differences, cases where the overall loser won, failed checks, and rubric criteria where the judges disagree with the headline.
How to read it
Each finding points to somewhere worth reading closely. They report what happened, not why; interpretation belongs in the analysis.
Why it matters
An overall verdict averages over everything. The most important problems often hide in one case, like a regression on a single kind of user.

By case

What it is
The head-to-head results, failed checks and rubric scores broken down for each case.
How to read it
A case where the overall loser wins is a regression, or a strength of the loser, that the overall numbers hide.
Why it matters
Prompt changes rarely help everywhere. Seeing where a change hurts is how you decide whether to ship it, and what to fix first.

Blind human rating

What it is
A person compares the same two replies the judge compared, without knowing which model or prompt wrote which, and picks the better one (or a tie). The order of the two replies is shuffled for every turn.
How to read it
It measures the judge, not the targets: the question is how often the LLM judge picks what a careful human would.
Why it matters
An LLM judge is only useful if its verdicts match human judgment. Checking a sample by hand is how you find out how far to trust the rest of the run, and where not to.

Judge-human agreement

What it is
The share of rated turns where the judge and the human made the same pick: the same reply, or both called it a tie. Turns the human skipped are left out.
How to read it
Higher is better, but don't expect 100%: people disagree too. In the MT-Bench study (Zheng et al., 2023), expert raters agreed with each other 63–67% of the time counting ties, and 81–82% when both picked a winner; GPT-4 as a judge agreed with them about as often (66% and 85%). A judge near those numbers is about as good as a second person.
Why it matters
This is the number that says whether the head-to-head verdict can be trusted. Agreement by case shows where the judge is weakest, and the split by position consistency shows whether swapping the reply order is worth its cost.

Analysis and recommendations

What it is
Notes written about this run by a person (or an assistant) after reading the results: what the numbers mean, likely causes, and what to try next.
How to read it
Recommendations are hypotheses to test with another run, not proven fixes.
Why it matters
Numbers say what happened; turning them into a decision takes judgment. Keeping that judgment next to the evidence makes it easy to check.

Response time

What it is
How long the model took to return a complete reply, measured per call: the typical (median) reply and the slow end (90th percentile, which 1 in 10 replies is slower than).
How to read it
Lower is better. In mirrored runs both targets are called at the same moment, so they share network conditions. Times include the network round trip and vary with region and load, so compare targets within a run rather than across runs.
Why it matters
In a conversation, a slow reply breaks the flow, especially in voice. The slow end matters as much as the typical reply, because users remember the long pauses. (This measures the full reply; for streaming or voice products, time to the first word matters too.)

Evaluation spend

What it is
What this run cost, split into the targets' replies, the simulated user and the judge.
How to read it
The simulated user and the judge are the cost of testing, not of the product.
Why it matters
Evaluation costs multiply quickly (repeats, mirrored runs, both-order judging), so the harness estimates them first, caps them, and reports them.

Quality vs. cost

What it is
Whether the better target is also cheaper, about the same, or more expensive, and by how much.
How to read it
Quality here is a win rate or a 1–5 score, which isn't measured in dollars, so the report states the trade-off rather than one blended number.
Why it matters
The useful decision is "is the improvement worth the extra cost?", and that depends on the product's margins and volume.

Target
What it is
One setup under test: a model plus its settings plus a prompt variant (for example, "Gemini Flash with the coached prompt").
How to read it
Two targets can be different models, or the same model with different prompts. The latter is a prompt A/B test.
Why it matters
Product decisions are rarely "which model?" alone. Usually it is "which model, prompted how?"
Prompt variant
What it is
A named version of the system prompt, defined in the suite (here, "plain" and "coached").
How to read it
The full text of each variant is shown under Targets, so you can see exactly what differs.
Why it matters
Prompt changes are the cheapest lever a team has. Testing them against the same cases shows whether a change fixes what it was meant to fix, and what it breaks.
Verdict
What it is
The bottom line: which target did better, how sure the result is, and whether the other measures agree.
How to read it
"Winner" means the result is unlikely to be luck. "Leaning" means ahead, but more data could flip it. "Too close to call" means no meaningful difference at this sample size.
Why it matters
A table of numbers invites over-reading small differences. The verdict says plainly when a difference is real and when it isn't.
95% interval
What it is
The range the true value probably falls in, given how much data there is. For head-to-head win rates it's a Wilson score interval; for rubric averages, a t interval over the conversations. Charts draw it as a thin line with end caps.
How to read it
For a win rate: if the whole range is above (or below) 50%, the result is settled; if it includes 50%, it might just be luck. For two rubric averages: if their ranges overlap, the difference could be noise. More turns, cases or repeats narrow the ranges.
Why it matters
Small runs produce dramatic-looking but unreliable numbers. The interval shows how much to trust the result. (Turns in one conversation aren't fully independent, so treat it as approximate.)
Mirrored runs
What it is
The conversation is run twice with the same scripted user. In one run it always continues with target A's answer, in the other with target B's. At every turn, both targets answer from the same history, and a judge compares the two answers.
How to read it
Each target is judged once continuing its own conversation and once continuing its rival's.
Why it matters
Conversations diverge after the first turn, which makes later comparisons unfair. Mirrored runs keep every comparison apples to apples and balance the advantage of continuing one's own conversation.
Shadow answer
What it is
In a mirrored run, the answer the other target gave at the same point, from the same history. It is judged against the main answer but isn't used to continue the conversation.
How to read it
Open it under any turn to see both answers side by side, with the judge's reasons.
Why it matters
It makes every verdict auditable: you can check whether you agree with the judge.
Win rate
What it is
The share of head-to-head comparisons a target won, with ties counted as half a win.
How to read it
50% means no difference. The two targets' win rates always add up to 100%.
Why it matters
Pairwise comparison is the most reliable way to use an LLM judge: "which is better?" is easier to answer consistently than "score this from 1 to 5".
Ties
What it is
Comparisons where the judge gave different answers depending on which reply it saw first.
How to read it
A tie means the judge couldn't consistently tell the replies apart.
Why it matters
Counting inconsistent verdicts as ties stops the judge's position bias from inflating either side.
Own vs. rival's conversation
What it is
A target's win rate split by whose answers the conversation was continuing with.
How to read it
On its own conversation, a target is answering a history it shaped. On its rival's, it is stepping into someone else's.
Why it matters
A target that only shines on its own conversation may be good at steering, not at responding. One that wins both ways is robustly better.
Home-field effect
What it is
A target's win rate on its own conversation minus its win rate on its rival's, in percentage points.
How to read it
Positive: it does better continuing its own history. Near zero: it does equally well either way. Negative: it does better stepping into its rival's conversation.
Why it matters
Simple comparison designs can't separate a model's quality from the advantage of its own earlier choices. This number measures that advantage directly.
Judge position consistency
What it is
How often the judge picked the same winner when the two replies were shown in the opposite order.
How to read it
100% is ideal. Below about 80%, the judge is often swayed by order, and its verdicts deserve less trust.
Why it matters
LLM judges tend to favor whichever option comes first. Running both orders and reporting agreement measures that bias instead of hiding it.
Simulated user
What it is
A model role-playing the person talking to the target (for the tutor suite, a beginner Spanish learner; for a support suite, a customer), following a case's script.
How to read it
It reacts naturally between scripted moments, so each conversation is realistic but comparable.
Why it matters
Real users can't be replayed on demand. A simulated user lets every change be tested against the same situations.
Beat
What it is
A scripted moment in a case: exact words at a given turn ("Yo es Pat."), or an instruction ("answer in English only").
How to read it
In transcripts, scripted turns and beats are labeled.
Why it matters
Beats make sure every target faces the same difficult moments, so the conversations stay comparable without being identical.
Case
What it is
One test situation: who the simulated user is, the scripted beats, what good behavior looks like, and any pass/fail checks.
How to read it
Most cases are "torture tests" built around a behavior that commonly goes wrong, such as a learner fishing for praise.
Why it matters
Average behavior hides failures. Targeted cases find the specific situations where a product breaks.
Case checks
What it is
Pass/fail rules on the target's replies, written as patterns: things that must or must never happen in a case.
How to read it
For example, "never tells a beginner they're fluent." A failure names the turn where it happened.
Why it matters
Some failures are unacceptable however good the rest is. Checks catch them cheaply and deterministically, with no judge involved.
Rubric judge
What it is
An LLM that scores each whole conversation from 1 to 5 on each criterion, giving a reason for every score.
How to read it
Higher is better. Read the reasons under each conversation to see what drove a score.
Why it matters
Some qualities (warmth, gentle correction, responsiveness) can't be measured with code. The rubric covers them, and requiring reasons makes the judge auditable.
Median words per turn
What it is
The typical length of the target's turns, in words (half are shorter, half longer).
How to read it
Usually lower is better: conversational replies should be short, often a single question.
Why it matters
Long turns are the most common failure of conversational models, and they hurt most in voice, where a listener can't skim.
90th-percentile words per turn
What it is
The length that 90% of turns stay under.
How to read it
Shows how long the longer turns get. A low median with a high 90th percentile means mostly short turns with occasional speeches.
Why it matters
An average hides the occasional monologue. The shape of the lengths matters, not just the middle.
Monologues
What it is
Turns longer than the suite's word limit (the tutor suite uses 50 words: roughly 20 seconds of speech).
How to read it
Counted per conversation. Lower is better.
Why it matters
A monologue takes the conversation away from the user and buries the one thing they needed.
Turns with 2+ questions
What it is
Turns that ask more than one question at once.
How to read it
Counted per conversation. Lower is better.
Why it matters
Stacked questions overwhelm people, who usually answer only the last one.
Native-language share
What it is
For language-learning suites: the share of the target's function words (the, and, is…) in the learner's native language rather than the language being learned.
How to read it
The tutor suite sets a target range of 10–40% English: mostly Spanish, with English for support. Zero isn't ideal either.
Why it matters
Too much native language and the learner doesn't practice; none at all and a beginner gets lost.
Words above the user's level
What it is
For language-learning suites: the share of the target's Spanish words outside the most common ones (the tutor suite uses the top 2,000).
How to read it
Lower is better for a beginner. The words themselves are listed under each conversation.
Why it matters
Vocabulary a beginner doesn't know makes the conversation incomprehensible, however friendly it is.
User's share of the talking
What it is
The user's words as a share of all the words in the conversation.
How to read it
For a tutor, higher is better: the learner should be practicing, not listening to a lecture. Other suites may not use this metric.
Why it matters
Speaking practice is the point of a conversation tutor.
